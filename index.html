Learning to learn: my academic journey so far

My journey into tertiary education has been a period of deliberate adjustment rather than a sudden transformation. When I began my Bachelor of Science in Business Intelligence and Data Analytics at Botswana School of Business Sciences, I arrived with a strong work ethic shaped by my schooling and by my previous work as a sales agent. What I did not yet have was a clear method for learning technical material independently. The first few months challenged me to move from being a passive recipient of information to an active owner of my development.

At school I had often been described as hardworking and motivated, but university has shown me that effort alone is not enough; effort must be directed. I learned to break large tasks into smaller deliverables, keep a weekly plan and review what I had understood at the end of each study session. Time management became practical rather than theoretical. I began to protect study blocks, prepare for classes in advance and use short daily targets to measure progress. Completing those targets gave me the same sense of achievement I used to feel when I met sales goals, and that familiarity helped me stay consistent.

Working as a sales agent before university taught me communication and resilience. I had to listen carefully, explain value clearly and remain composed when a day did not go as planned. Those skills have transferred directly into my academic work. Group discussions are easier when I can listen without interrupting, ask clarifying questions and help the team stay focused on the agreed output. I also discovered that problem solving is rarely a solo activity; explaining a problem to someone else often exposes the part I had not yet understood.

The most meaningful growth has come from my interest in technical and analytical work. I enjoy taking raw information and shaping it into something that supports a decision. My Python practice, including a match-prediction model built with Elo ratings, rolling form, head-to-head records and odds data, showed me that a result is only as good as the questions asked of the data. It also taught me to read documentation, test small pieces of code and accept that debugging is part of learning rather than a sign of failure.

There have been difficult weeks. Balancing coursework, personal development and the desire to gain practical experience sometimes felt like competing priorities. Instead of abandoning my plan, I started keeping a simple learning log: what I studied, what confused me and what I would do differently next time. That habit turned reflection into a study strategy. It also reminded me that discipline is not punishment; it is the structure that makes continuous learning possible.

Looking ahead, I want to deepen my technical skills, complete relevant certificates and secure practical experience through an attachment or internship. I know I still need to improve my confidence when presenting technical work and my speed when working with unfamiliar libraries. However, I am no longer intimidated by that gap. My academic journey so far has taught me that I can learn, adapt and contribute when I stay disciplined, curious and willing to work with others.

What working in a group taught me about communication

Tuckman's stages of group development give me a useful language for describing how our Communication and Study Skills group changed over the semester. What began as a set of individuals with different routines became a team that could disagree, make decisions and still meet a shared deadline.

Forming. In the beginning, we were polite and cautious. We did not all know one another beforehand, so early conversations focused on introductions, clarifying the task and checking what each person expected. I remember feeling relieved that everyone took the work seriously, but also aware that we had not yet agreed on how we would work together. At that point, I contributed by asking practical questions: What exactly must we submit? When is each part due? Who is responsible for which section?

Storming. This stage arrived gradually. The main tension was not hostility; it was the friction between different schedules and different ideas about how much detail was enough. Some members wanted to move quickly to a draft, while others wanted more discussion before writing. I noticed that my first instinct was to take on more work myself to keep things moving. That was not helpful, because it removed responsibility from the team. We resolved it by naming the issue openly and agreeing to decide using the assignment rubric rather than personal preference. That shift lowered the temperature and made disagreements useful.

Norming. During this stage, we built shared routines. We used one folder, agreed on simple file names, set short check-in times and confirmed who would do what by when. We also learned to give feedback on the work without judging the person. Once those norms were in place, I found it easier to say "I don't understand this part" or "Could we make this clearer?" without worrying about causing offence.

Performing. By this stage, the group was functioning well. People brought different strengths: some organised the structure, some checked language and some tested ideas against the requirements. My role was often to pull the pieces together and check that the final message was clear for the reader. The clearest sign of performing was that we could still disagree about a sentence and then resolve it quickly because we trusted the process we had built.

Adjourning. Closing the task brought a mix of relief and reflection. After submission, we took a moment to acknowledge what had worked and what we would change. There was no major unresolved tension, but we agreed that starting our final checks earlier would have reduced pressure. That honest closing conversation mattered to me; it turned the end of the task into a learning moment rather than just a finish line.

The most important lesson I took from this experience is that teamwork is not the absence of disagreement. It is the presence of a respectful process for handling it. I became more confident in asking clarifying questions, more disciplined in keeping commitments and more aware that listening is an active contribution. Those lessons will stay with me as I move into more technical and project-based work, where collaboration will be just as important as code.

Optional · Study Tips & Resources

Strategies that support continuous learning.

01

Use focused blocks

Study in short, timed sessions with deliberate breaks. I use focused blocks for reading, coding practice and revision so that each session has a clear purpose.

02

Practise active recall

Close the notes and explain the idea in your own words. If you cannot explain it simply, that is the part to revisit.

03

Learn by building

Small projects make technical concepts stick. Rebuilding a feature from one of my own projects teaches more than passively watching a tutorial.

04

Keep a learning log

Record what you studied, what confused you and the next action. This turns reflection into a practical study tool.

05

Use teach-back

Explain technical work to someone without a technical background. It improves communication and reveals gaps in understanding.
